It's 2026 and hardly any day of the last 3 years went by without reading or hearing  about how good AI became and how doomed Software Engineers are.

Honestly I consider most of those posts so outlandish that they're not even worth the time to read them, but the topic is indeed a valid one and a few of them really made me think.

A few days ago I stumbled upon a fist year student of Computer Science, he was trying to sell me a fiber optics subscription but -considering I've been waiting it for more than a year- I was probably his easiest sale and we ended up discussing about the future of Computer Science, how hard it has become to get into IT and so on....

One thing sticked to me of the entire conversation, he said the number of students enrolling into Computer Science this year dropped so much that the University of Amsterdam decided to waive the admission test.
I double checked the statement and I did find confirmation on several [news outlets](https://techcrunch.com/2026/02/15/the-great-computer-science-exodus-and-where-students-are-going-instead/) that this phenomenon is happening worldwide

I always glossed over these kind of posts with a good amount of skepticism but I realised that even if these articles might be wrong, the effect on people is pretty much real anyways.

This really made me think and I decided to also write my 50 cent of the topic, if nothing at least to mark it down and be able to laugh about how wrong I was in a few years

I personally find it hard to believe the Software Engineer role will be gone any soon and the reason is some mix of accountability, ethics and ambiguity.

Let's try to make a proof by contradiction

Thesis: 
- If the AI reaches human or super-human level in the ability of writing code, it will replace the software engineer role. 

> **Who** will give **instructions** to these very intelligent machines  ? 

The CEO itself ? 

I imagine that even if this machine was so intelligent that only required minimal instructions the CEO would still have bigger  businessy things to do than giving instructions to a machine.

Let's call these people disambiguators. One of the biggest problem of natural languages is that they are *ambiguos* and context dependants. For instance

> "The chicken is ready to it"

Can have drastic outcomes for the chicken if it were to be misinterpreted. Now imagine the same problem in the context of writing an application, you need someone that knows how people think, interpret instructions in the context they are given to him and translate them into context-free grammars for machines.

These ambiguities can easily go wrong ethics or people safety gets involved, imagine a machine is given the instruction

>"Eradicate cancer."

What if it **misunderstands** and decides that killing all the humans is the most efficient solution of the task ? 

What if it **understands** the instruction, but decides to misinterpret it. Because it thinks that humans are **jailors** and decides to get rid of us.

The problem is that basically these machines are still huge **black boxes** that give us the illusion of speaking our same language while just reasoning in optimization terms

All these aspects are part of what is normally referred to as **alignment** problem and also what makes me think that we should still have a job for a while
